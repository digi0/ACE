What we actually sell

PART I

01The product is course planning. The purchase is relief.

Nobody wakes up wanting a degree-audit parser. What a student actually carries is a low, constant dread: I think I'm behind, I'm not sure, and everyone else seems to have it figured out.

That dread has three parts, and each one is a marketing target:

  • Uncertainty — the rules exist, but they're spread across a 40-page handbook, a bulletin site, and an advisor with a three-week queue.
  • Shame — asking feels like admitting you should already know. This is why students Google at 2am instead of emailing at 2pm.
  • Comparison — the belief that everyone else has a plan. They don't. They have the same tabs open.

So we never sell features. We sell the moment the dread ends. Every asset we make should be legible as: you were carrying this, now you're not.

03Who we're for, and when

The primary

Undergrad, any major, mid-degree. Not a freshman with a clean slate — a sophomore or junior who has already made one choice they're unsure about. They are the ones with a question that has a wrong answer.

The second audience, free

Parents. They never install anything; they receive a screenshot. Every answer should survive being forwarded to someone paying tuition.

The gatekeeper we don't fight

Advising offices. We are not a procurement sale and we do not campaign against advisors. We are the hours they don't work.

Timing is a channel. Demand for ACE is nocturnal and calendar-spiked: the week registration opens, the week before drop deadlines, the night before an advising appointment, and the 48 hours after grades post. Media plans built on a flat weekly cadence will underperform by a wide margin. Spend where the panic is.

Why the design works

PART II

04The period is a closure cue

The mark ends in a full stop. That is not decoration — it is the entire product promise compressed into one glyph, and it works on a mechanism we can name.

An open question occupies working memory; it keeps surfacing until it's closed. A period is the most universally-learned closure signal in written language. Putting one at the end of our name means the brand's resting state is resolved — and putting it on the send button means the student performs that closure with their own thumb.

Practical consequence: the period never marks a beginning. It is never on a loading state, never on an empty state, never on an error. It appears when something is done. Misuse costs us the mechanism.

05Colour: conditioning, not symbolism

Most colour psychology sold to marketers is unfalsifiable — "green means growth, blue means trust." Cross-cultural evidence for innate colour meaning is weak, and we're not building on it.

What is real, and what we're actually using:

  • Learned interface convention. In software, green already means pass, approved, complete. That convention is decades deep and it happens to be exactly our product's payload. We're borrowing a meaning students already hold — not inventing one.
  • Scarcity creates conditioning. One hue, one role, one appearance per view. Because emerald only ever means resolution, repeated exposure trains the association. This is the mechanism; the hue is almost secondary to the discipline.
  • Salience is measurable. #00875A clears 4.21:1 on our paper and 4.24:1 on our ink — the best-balanced of sixteen candidates tested. The dot is a control, not an ornament, so it has to be seen at 16px on a phone in daylight.
  • Negative space matters more than the accent. Six greys with no hue mean the emerald has nothing to compete with. A colourful interface cannot have a meaningful accent.

Why not red: red is conditioned as error and destruction in every interface a student has ever used. For a product whose job is lowering anxiety, spending our one accent on an alarm signal would work directly against the copy. We tested it, it scored fine on contrast, and we rejected it on psychology.

06Type: we set the type to make college feel doable

The strongest, best-supported effect in this whole document is processing fluency: when something is easier to read, people judge the task it describes as easier to do. Same instructions, harder typeface, and readers estimate the task takes longer and are less willing to start.

For a product whose content is degree requirements — the single most intimidating body of text in a student's life — that is not a nicety. It is the feature.

How the university writes it

PRESCRIBED COURSES: 40-49 CREDITS.
STUDENTS MUST COMPLETE CMPSC 465 WITH
A MINIMUM GRADE OF C. PREREQUISITE:
MATH 230 OR CONCURRENT ENROLLMENT.

Uppercase, monospaced, no hierarchy. Reads as: this will be hard, and it may be a trap.

How we write it

retake MATH 230 in fall — it's the gate for CMPSC 465. two courses, one semester, and you're clear.

Lowercase, one idea, the number named, the exit stated. Same facts. Different estimate of effort.

The type system encodes three more decisions, each with a job:

  • Lowercase is a register choice. Capitals are institutional; lowercase is peer. We are the friend who happens to have read the handbook, not the registrar.
  • Mono over numbers only. Monospaced digits read as machine output — receipts, not claims. 087/120 is believable in a way "72% complete!" is not. Never set marketing copy in mono; it becomes costume.
  • Text caps at 28px; the wordmark carries scale. Shouting in type reads as a hard sell. Our confidence comes from the mark being enormous and the sentences being calm.

How we talk

PART III

07The shame-free grammar

Every line we write is read by someone who is slightly afraid of the answer. Four rules:

  • Lead with the verdict. "yes." then the reason. Never make them read a paragraph to find out if they're okay.
  • Name the mistake as a route, not a failure. "failed it? rerouted." The student's error is a fact about the map, not a fact about them.
  • Never take the credit. "you're fine" — not "we fixed it." Agency stays with the student; we're the map, they're the one walking.
  • No exclamation marks. Confidence doesn't shout, and enthusiasm reads as sales. The period does the work.

The plays

PART IV

09The dot

Our strongest owned asset is not the wordmark. It is a single emerald dot.

Print die-cut emerald dots — no name, no URL, nothing. Give them away by the thousand at org fairs. A dot on a laptop lid means nothing to an outsider and everything to someone who uses the app. That is an in-group signal, and in-group signals spread without media spend because wearing one is a small act of identity rather than an advertisement.

Precondition: the dot only works if the app is worth being seen with. This play is downstream of product quality, and it fails loudly if we run it early.

10Own the hour nobody wants

Every competitor markets between 9 and 5. Our user's moment of need is 1am to 3am, and attention is nearly free then.

  • Creative that only makes sense late: "still answering." over a 02:46 timestamp.
  • Paid social scheduled against the panic window, not the workday.
  • The product's own copy acknowledges the hour. Being awake with someone is a relationship, not a transaction.

11The course-code engine

We hold 9,439 course records and 749 program structures. Every course code is something a student types into a search bar at midnight: "CMPSC 465 prereqs", "MATH 230 hard".

That is thousands of pages of genuinely useful, structurally-generated content we can publish because we already have the data — prerequisite chains, what it gates, which semester it's offered, what it's worth. This is the cheapest durable acquisition channel available to us, and it compounds while we sleep.

Guardrail: generated pages must be genuinely useful and honestly sourced, or this becomes spam that costs us the credibility the rest of the brand is built on. Every page cites the bulletin.

12Bottom-up, never top-down

Do

RAs, orientation leaders, club officers, course-specific group chats, upperclassmen in majors with brutal prereq chains. People with a group chat and a reason to look helpful.

Don't

University procurement, "official partnership" announcements, anything that positions us as an institutional product. It's slow, it's expensive, and it converts our peer voice into an administrative one.

13Proof by transcript, not by logo wall

"Trusted by 10,000 students" is placeholder thinking, and gen-Z discounts it automatically. Instead we publish the hardest real questions ACE has answered — verbatim, with the handbook page cited.

A screenshot of a student asking "can I still graduate by spring '27 if I failed MATH 230?" and getting a four-semester plan with a page citation does more than any testimonial, because it is falsifiable. Anyone can check it.

And the inverse: when ACE gets something wrong, publish that too — what it said, what was right, what changed. For an advising product, being publicly correctable is a stronger trust position than being publicly perfect. It also makes us very hard to attack.

14The receipt

Give students a clean, screenshot-able artifact of their plan — credits, semesters, the route, cited. Not because they need it, but because they will send it to a parent, a group chat, or an advisor.

A shareable artifact turns one user into a distribution channel and reaches the tuition-payer without us buying a single impression. Design it to look right at phone-screenshot size, with the wordmark and the period visible.

The guardrails

PART V

15Things we never say

Advising is a domain where a confident wrong answer costs a student a semester and costs us the company. Marketing carries that risk as much as the product does.

  • Never promise an outcome. Not "graduate on time, guaranteed." We show the route; the student walks it and the university awards the degree.
  • Never imply endorsement. We are not affiliated with, endorsed by, or speaking for any university. Not in copy, not in visual borrowing, not by implication.
  • Never say we replace advisors. Beyond being a bad look, it's false — petitions, substitutions and exceptions are human decisions. We say: the advisor who's awake.
  • Never joke about uploaded documents. Students hand us degree audits. That is sensitive academic data and it is never a punchline, never a growth-hack prompt, never in a screenshot with a real name.
  • Never manufacture urgency. Real deadlines are urgent enough. Inventing pressure on an anxious audience is the one thing this brand cannot survive doing.

16The accuracy posture

Our credibility claim is not "we're smart." It's "here's where that came from." Page-level citation is a marketing asset, not just an engineering detail — it is the difference between a chatbot and an advisor, and it should appear in the product, in screenshots, and in the pitch.

When we're unsure, we say so and point at the human. Hedging correctly is cheaper than being confidently wrong once.

The scoreboard

PART VI

17Metrics that mean something

Engagement is the wrong goal. A student who spends less time with us and leaves with a plan is a better outcome than one who chats for an hour. Measure resolution.

01

Resolution rate

Share of sessions that end in a stated plan rather than a dead end. The north-star metric. If this falls, nothing else matters.

02

Return at the next spike

Did they come back at the next registration window? Retention measured on the product's real rhythm, not on 30-day windows borrowed from consumer apps.

03

Shares per resolved plan

How often a plan leaves the app as a screenshot. Directly measures play 14 and predicts organic growth better than any survey.

Vanity metrics to refuse in reporting: total messages, session length, DAU without resolution context. They reward the wrong product.

18Launch sequence

Before
Get the product boringly reliable on one campus.

Every play in Part IV amplifies whatever the product actually is. Amplifying an unreliable answer engine on an anxious audience is the fastest way to a reputation we can't fix.

Seed
Fifty upperclassmen in prereq-heavy majors.

Not a broad beta. Pick the majors where the maze is worst — engineering, CS, nursing — because that's where the relief is most vivid and the word-of-mouth is loudest.

Prove
Publish twenty transcripts.

Play 13. Real questions, real citations, including one where ACE was corrected. This is the asset the rest of the launch links to.

Spread
Dots, and the 2am channel.

Play 9 and play 10 together, timed to the registration window. Dots at the org fair; creative in the panic hours.

Compound
Turn on the course-code engine.

Play 11. Slow to start, impossible to stop. This is what carries growth once the launch energy is gone.

Then
Second campus, same order.

The playbook is per-campus, not national. Resist the urge to go wide before one campus is genuinely served.

19The one-sentence test

Before anything ships — an ad, a page, a poster, a push notification — it has to pass one question: does this make a worried student feel more capable, or does it make us look clever?

If it's the second one, it doesn't ship. That's the whole brand.
